Colour guide

How to Choose Your Brand Colours

Colour is often the first thing people remember about a brand. Here is how to choose a palette that fits your business and works everywhere.

Why colour matters

Colour makes a brand recognisable before anyone reads a word. A consistent palette across your packaging, website, social posts and signage builds recognition over time. Colour also sets the mood: soft, muted tones feel calm and premium, while bright, saturated colours feel energetic and playful.

Colour meaning: useful, not a rulebook

You will often read that blue means trust or red means hunger. These associations are loose and vary between cultures and industries, so treat them as a starting point rather than a rule. What matters more is whether the colour suits your customer, your price point and your category, and whether it makes you look different from the businesses around you.

Structure of a brand palette

  • Primary colour: the one people should associate with you. Used for the logo and key moments.
  • Secondary colours: two or three that support the primary in layouts, packaging and graphics.
  • Neutrals: off-whites, greys or deep tones for backgrounds and text.
  • Accent: one bright colour for buttons and calls to action.

Layla Flowers, for example, pairs a deep navy with a soft blush and a gold accent, a combination that feels romantic and premium on wrapping paper, ribbons and tags.

Check your competitors

Line up the logos and Instagram feeds of five competitors. If they all use the same colours, choosing something different is one of the easiest ways to stand out on a crowded shelf or feed.

Make it readable

Text needs strong contrast against its background to be readable, especially on phones in bright sunlight. Test your colours together, and avoid light text on light backgrounds or colours that are hard to tell apart for people with colour blindness.

Record the exact codes

Every colour should have a HEX and RGB code for screens, CMYK for print and, ideally, a Pantone reference for packaging and signage. Without them, your colour will drift from printer to printer and screen to screen. These codes belong in your brand guidelines.

A simple process for choosing your palette

  1. Re-read your one-page brand strategy and the three to five words describing how customers should feel.
  2. Collect colours from images that capture that feeling, such as interiors, fashion, nature or packaging you admire.
  3. Compare them with your competitors and remove anything too close.
  4. Pick one primary colour, then two or three supporting colours and neutrals.
  5. Test the palette on real mock-ups: a social post, a bag, a website button and a sign.
  6. Print samples before committing to large packaging runs.

Colour in the UAE market

Dubai's strong sunlight, bright interiors and busy streets affect how colours read. Very pale colours can wash out on outdoor signage, while deep colours like navy, forest green and maroon stay rich and legible. Gold and warm neutrals are popular in premium gifting and hospitality, which makes them feel familiar but also harder to stand out with. If your competitors all use gold, a confident contrasting colour can make you memorable.

Using colour consistently

A palette only builds recognition if it is used the same way everywhere. Decide roughly how much of each colour to use, which colour goes on buttons, and which backgrounds text sits on. Write these rules into your guidelines so your social media, packaging and website all look like the same brand.

Frequently asked questions

How many brand colours should I have?

Usually one primary, two or three secondary colours, a couple of neutrals and one accent. More than that becomes hard to use consistently.

Can I change my brand colours later?

Yes, but every change costs recognition. It is better to choose carefully at the start and refine gradually.

Do colours look the same in print and on screen?

No. That is why you need separate RGB and CMYK codes, and why printed samples should be checked before a large print run.
