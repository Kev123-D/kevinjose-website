Brand Guidelines: What to Include

Brand guidelines are the instruction manual that keeps your brand consistent, whoever is creating something for you.

By Kevin Jose5 min readDubai, UAE

In this guide

  2. What a brand book should include
  3. Keep it usable
  4. Update it as you grow
  5. Logo rules in detail
  6. Tone of voice

Why you need guidelines

As soon as more than one person creates things for your business, such as a printer, a social media manager, a web developer or a new staff member, your brand starts to drift. Guidelines stop that. They also make every future project faster, because the decisions are already made.

What a brand book should include

  • Brand story and strategy: who you are for, what you stand for and your one clear difference.
  • Logo usage: versions, minimum size, clear space and examples of what not to do.
  • Colour palette: HEX, RGB, CMYK and Pantone codes, and how much of each colour to use.
  • Typography: heading and body fonts, sizes and an Arabic font if needed.
  • Photography style: light, composition, editing and example images.
  • Graphic elements: patterns, icons and shapes.
  • Tone of voice: how you write, with example captions and phrases to avoid.
  • Applications: examples on packaging, signage, social posts and the website.

Keep it usable

A good brand book is short enough that people actually read it. Lead with the essentials on the first few pages, use plenty of visual examples, and share it as a PDF that can be sent to any supplier.

Update it as you grow

Add new applications, such as a vehicle livery or a new product line, as the brand expands. When Sama Beirut added a delivery van and bread bags, the guidelines made sure they matched the existing signage and packaging.

Logo rules in detail

The logo section is the most used part of any brand book. Show every approved version, the minimum size it can be used at, the clear space around it and which version to use on light, dark and photographic backgrounds. Include a page of mistakes to avoid, such as stretching, recolouring, adding shadows or placing it on busy images, because showing what not to do is often clearer than describing it.

Tone of voice

How you write is as much a part of your brand as how you look. Describe your voice in three words, for example warm, confident and simple, and give examples of real sentences in that voice: a welcome message, an Instagram caption, a reply to a complaint. If you serve customers in Arabic and English, note how the voice should carry across both.

Digital and print essentials

Include practical details that suppliers ask for: colour codes for screen and print, file formats, social media profile and post templates, email signature design, website button styles and print specifications. The more practical the guide, the more consistently it gets used.

Sharing and storing your brand files

Keep the guidelines and every logo, font licence and template together in one shared folder, with clear file names. When a new supplier or staff member starts, sending them the guidelines and folder link is often all they need to produce on-brand work.

Frequently asked questions

How long should brand guidelines be?

Small businesses usually need a concise guide covering logo, colours, fonts, photography and tone. Larger brands add more applications over time.

Who should get a copy?

Anyone who creates something for you: designers, printers, agencies, web developers and staff handling social media.

Can guidelines be created for an existing brand?

Yes. Guidelines can document and tidy up a brand that already exists.
